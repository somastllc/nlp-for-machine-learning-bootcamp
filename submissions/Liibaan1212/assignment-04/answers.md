# Part A – Research

## 1. Text vectorization and one-hot encoding

**Text vectorization** is the process of converting text (words, sentences, or documents) into numerical features that a machine-learning algorithm can process. Most traditional machine-learning estimators expect a rectangular feature matrix: every sample must have the same number of columns, and column *j* must have the same meaning for every sample. A document with a different number of features from another document would not fit the same fixed model input structure.

In **one-hot encoding**, each vocabulary item receives its own position in a vector. The position for the selected word is `1`; every other position is `0`. For the vocabulary `[cash, class, free, lunch, notes, win]`, `free` becomes `[0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0]`. One-hot encoding a word is not, by itself, a complete representation of a multiword document: a sentence contains several word occurrences, so it produces several word vectors, or those vectors must be combined into a document-level representation. For example, the message “win free cash” produces three separate six-dimensional vectors. A simple way to combine them is to add them, producing one count vector.

**Real-life example:** a spam detector cannot directly learn from the raw message “Claim your free prize.” It can instead map the message into a fixed set of numerical features such as the counts of `claim`, `free`, and `prize`, allowing the same model input columns to be used for every email.

## 2. Bag of Words and the document-term matrix

**Bag of Words (BoW)** represents each document by counting how often each vocabulary term appears. The **document-term matrix (DTM)** is the resulting two-dimensional table:

- Each **row** represents one document.
- Each **column** represents one vocabulary term (or, in an n-gram model, one vocabulary feature).
- Each **cell** contains that term's count in that document. A binary BoW variant stores only whether the term occurs.

It is called a “bag” because the basic representation keeps the words and their counts but discards their original sequence. “win free phone” and “phone free win” therefore have identical count vectors. Losing order may do little harm for a broad topic-classification task such as identifying whether a news article is about sports or technology, because topic words can be useful even without exact order. It can cause serious problems in sentiment or intent classification: “the food was good” and “the food was not good” contain nearly the same individual words, but they express different opinions. BoW unigrams do not represent the negation relationship.

## 3. Vocabulary size and sparsity

The **vocabulary** is the set of distinct tokens learned from the training corpus. Its size is the number of columns in a word-level DTM. A real corpus may contain thousands or millions of distinct terms, while each individual document uses only a small fraction of them. Consequently, most document-term cells are zero: this is **sparsity**.

A sparse matrix format stores non-zero values and the information needed to locate them, rather than storing every zero. Formats such as CSR (Compressed Sparse Row) are efficient for many machine-learning operations. scikit-learn's text vectorizers return sparse matrices by default, avoiding the memory cost of converting a very wide DTM into a dense array.

Vocabulary controls change which features are retained:

- `min_df` removes terms appearing in fewer than the specified minimum number or proportion of documents. It can remove rare typos and one-off names, but may also remove rare yet important spam indicators.
- `max_df` removes terms appearing in more than the specified maximum number or proportion of documents. This can remove corpus-wide terms that are unlikely to distinguish documents.
- `max_features` keeps only the most frequent features, subject to the other settings. It limits memory and feature count but can discard useful rare terms.
- **Lowercasing** merges case variants such as `Win` and `WIN` into `win`.
- **Stop-word removal** removes selected common words. It can reduce noise, but an unsuitable stop list can remove meaningful words such as `not`.
- **Stemming** reduces related word forms to a common stem; **lemmatization** maps inflected forms to a dictionary base form. These can reduce vocabulary size, but may also merge forms whose distinction matters.

## 4. N-grams

An **n-gram** is a contiguous sequence of *n* tokens. A unigram is one token (`not`); a bigram is two adjacent tokens (`not good`); a trigram is three (`the food was`). N-grams preserve some local word order while still producing fixed vocabulary features.

For example, the bigram `not good` preserves a negative phrase that unigram features `not` and `good` cannot connect. Larger n-grams can capture more context, but they create many more possible features. They are less likely to repeat across documents, increase memory and computation, and may overfit when the training corpus is small. The best range depends on the task and should be selected using training-only validation.

## 5. TF-IDF

**TF-IDF** means *term frequency × inverse document frequency*. Term frequency (TF) measures how often a term occurs in a document. Inverse document frequency (IDF) reduces the weight of terms that occur in many documents and increases the relative importance of terms found in fewer documents.

A widely taught textbook form is:

\[
\operatorname{tfidf}(t,d)=\operatorname{tf}(t,d)\times\operatorname{idf}(t),
\qquad \operatorname{idf}(t)=\log\left(\frac{N}{\operatorname{df}(t)}\right)
\]

Here, `N` is the number of documents in the corpus and `df(t)` is the number of documents containing term `t`. The inverse-document-frequency idea is strongly associated with Karen Spärck Jones's 1972 paper, *A Statistical Interpretation of Term Specificity and Its Application in Retrieval*. She argued that terms occurring in fewer documents are statistically more specific and can be more useful for distinguishing relevant documents. The idea is about statistical distribution, not a claim that a rare word is inherently meaningful.

With the default settings, scikit-learn's `TfidfVectorizer` uses a smoothed IDF:

\[
\operatorname{idf}_{sk}(t)=\log\left(\frac{1+N}{1+\operatorname{df}(t)}\right)+1
\]

It multiplies the term frequency by this IDF and, by default, applies **L2 normalization** to each document vector:

\[
\hat{v}=\frac{v}{\sqrt{\sum_j v_j^2}}
\]

Smoothing adds one to the document count and document frequency, behaving as though an extra document contained every term once; this avoids problematic zero-frequency cases and moderates IDF values. The `norm='l2'` default makes each non-zero document vector have Euclidean length 1. Therefore, scikit-learn's displayed values will not generally equal the raw textbook TF-IDF numbers.

TF-IDF values are **weights, not probabilities**. They are not constrained to sum to one as a probability distribution, and they do not express the probability that a word or document belongs to a class. They are numerical importance weights based on term and corpus frequencies.

## 6. `fit`, `transform`, `fit_transform`, OOV words, and leakage

- `fit(documents)` learns information from the supplied documents. For a `CountVectorizer`, this includes the vocabulary and feature-to-column mapping. For a `TfidfVectorizer`, it also learns document-frequency-based IDF weights.
- `transform(documents)` applies the already learned vocabulary and weighting rules to new documents.
- `fit_transform(documents)` learns the representation and transforms those same documents in one operation.

An **out-of-vocabulary (OOV)** word is a token that was not in the fitted vocabulary. A standard scikit-learn vectorizer ignores it during `transform`; it does not create a new column because doing so would change the feature space expected by the model.

**Data leakage** occurs when information from the held-out test set is used while learning the representation or model. If a vectorizer is fitted on both training and test documents, it can learn test-set vocabulary and document frequencies. This allows the training process to use information about the test distribution, making the evaluation less faithful to the real situation in which future unseen data arrives. It may not use test labels, but it still contaminates the evaluation protocol.

Correct order:

1. Split the raw labeled data into training and test sets.
2. Fit the vectorizer on training text only.
3. Transform training text with that fitted vectorizer (or use `fit_transform` on training text).
4. Fit the classifier on the training vectors and labels.
5. Transform test text with the same fitted vectorizer.
6. Evaluate predictions on the test set.

For cross-validation, put the vectorizer and classifier in a scikit-learn `Pipeline`, so each fold learns its vocabulary and IDF from that fold's training portion only.

## 7. African-language case study: Hausa hate-speech detection

Sulaiman and Ahmad (2026), in *Hausa Hate Speech Detection Using Logistic Regression*, studied binary hate-speech detection using the Hausa subset of the AFRIHATE corpus. The paper reports 6,644 annotated social-media tweets; hate and abusive labels were combined into one positive class, with normal text as the other class. The authors compared **Bag of Words** and **TF-IDF** features with Logistic Regression and Random Forest, evaluating with stratified five-fold cross-validation. They report that Logistic Regression with TF-IDF achieved 94% accuracy and 93% F1, compared with 92% accuracy and 92% F1 for Logistic Regression with BoW. TF-IDF also outperformed BoW for Random Forest in their reported results. The authors describe text cleaning that included lowercasing, removing URLs and punctuation, Hausa stop-word removal, normalization, and stemming/lemmatization.

This case study shows that the choice of lexical weighting can affect a real African-language NLP system: TF-IDF's down-weighting of widely distributed terms helped the tested classifiers perform better than raw BoW in this experiment. However, neither BoW nor TF-IDF understands a word's meaning in the human sense. Both primarily represent token occurrence and distribution; basic unigrams do not inherently understand synonymy, context, sarcasm, or how negation changes a phrase. The study's results do **not** prove that TF-IDF understands semantics; they show that frequency-based lexical features can be useful baselines for a classification task. The paper itself contrasts these approaches with deep-learning and contextual approaches that can model more sequential or semantic patterns.

**Critical reading note:** this is one published experiment, not a universal ranking. Its reported scores depend on the corpus, preprocessing, labels, model settings, and evaluation design; they should not be assumed to transfer unchanged to Somali or another dataset.

### Task 11 – Data leakage

Fitting on D1–D4 gives a training vocabulary that does not contain the new test-only terms. Fitting on D1–D4 plus N1 and N2 adds `is`, `moved`, `prize`, `tomorrow`, and `your`. Those five words are the leaked test-set vocabulary information. The correct approach is to fit only on training documents and then call `transform` on the test documents.

### Task 12 – Why BoW does not capture meaning

The review vectors share the words `the`, `food`, and `was`; each review has one distinct opinion word (`tasty`, `delicious`, or `terrible`). The cosine similarity matrix is:

```text
[[1.00 0.75 0.75]
 [0.75 1.00 0.75]
 [0.75 0.75 1.00]]
```

R1 (`tasty`) is **not** more similar to R2 (`delicious`) than to R3 (`terrible`): both similarities are 0.75. BoW treats the distinct opinion words as unrelated dimensions and does not know that `tasty` and `delicious` are synonyms or that `terrible` expresses an opposite sentiment. This is a clear example of lexical overlap being different from semantic similarity.

## Spam detector: recommended vectorizer choices

For a first spam detector, I would compare word-level **TF-IDF** and raw **count** features using a validation split, rather than assuming one always wins. TF-IDF can emphasize distinctive terms: in this small corpus, `cash`, `call`, `entry`, and `prizes` receive larger D2 weights than words shared by more documents. I would test unigrams with bigrams because phrases such as `free entry` or `claim now` may be useful, but I would avoid large n-gram ranges at the start because the feature vocabulary grows quickly (32 unigrams, 67 unigram-plus-bigram features, and 98 features through trigrams even in this tiny corpus). I would not blindly remove English stop words: the experiment shows that the built-in list removes `not`, which can reverse or weaken an important message signal. I would begin without aggressive `min_df` or `max_features` limits, then tune them using training-only cross-validation; rare words can be highly informative in spam detection. All vectorizer fitting and tuning must happen inside the training pipeline to prevent leakage.
